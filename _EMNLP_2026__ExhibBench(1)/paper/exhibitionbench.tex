\begin{abstract}
Museum curation, selecting, sequencing, and contextualising artefacts
into a coherent exhibition, demands deep cultural knowledge, temporal
reasoning, and stylistic sensitivity.
We introduce \bench{}, the first benchmark designed to probe these
capabilities in large language models (LLMs).
\bench{} aggregates \textbf{300 real exhibitions} and \textbf{23{,}658 objects}
from five public collections spanning Western, East Asian, African,
and Islamic cultural traditions, and defines three tasks:
\textbf{\meip{}} (Masked Exhibition Item Prediction, 1{,}409 queries),
\textbf{\tes{}} (Theme-based Exhibition Selection, 283 queries),
and \textbf{\ecd{}} (Exhibition Coherence Discrimination, 500 pairwise samples).
We evaluate \textbf{28 systems} (21 frontier LLMs, 7 open-weight models, and 3 retrieval baselines) and report four key findings:
(1) few-shot gains are heterogeneous (Gemini-2.5-Pro $+2.5\%$; GPT-5.2 saturated at zero-shot);
(2) chain-of-thought hurts ECD universally ($-14$--$-34$\,pp Macro);
(3) cultural bias is nuanced: LLMs show negative $\Delta_{\mathrm{W{-}NW}}$ (higher accuracy on non-Western objects) while SBERT shows positive $\Delta$, suggesting corpus quality confounds bias measurement; and
(4) coherence insensitivity at L3 Thematic Deviation (best $0.63$) despite strong L1 performance ($0.98$).
All data, code, and baselines are publicly released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Curating an exhibition requires integrating art-historical knowledge,
cultural sensitivity, and narrative logic to assemble objects that
educate and move an audience, a process that currently demands weeks
of expert work \citep{macdonald2011companion}.
LLMs have shown strong performance on culturally grounded question
answering \citep{hendrycks2020mmlu} and text-based retrieval
\citep{nogueira2019passage}, making them appealing candidates for
curation assistance.
Yet \emph{no NLP benchmark} evaluates whether LLMs genuinely understand
exhibition-level coherence, curatorial logic, or cross-object contextual
dependencies.

We introduce \bench{}, a triple-task benchmark grounded in 300 real
exhibition records drawn from five major open-access public collections,
encompassing 23{,}658 catalogue objects.
\bench{} operationalises cultural anchoring across three complementary
dimensions: masked object prediction (\meip{}), theme-based exhibition
selection (\tes{}), and coherence discrimination (\ecd{}).
We evaluate 21 frontier LLMs, 7 open-weight models, and three retrieval baselines under a unified
zero- and few-shot protocol, constituting the most comprehensive evaluation
of LLMs in a cultural curation context to date.

Our evaluation reveals four key findings that challenge prevailing
assumptions about LLM cultural competence:
few-shot gains on object retrieval are model-heterogeneous;
chain-of-thought prompting universally degrades coherence judgments;
cultural bias measurement is confounded by corpus quality and sample size, with LLMs showing higher accuracy on non-Western objects; and frontier LLMs remain near-chance on subtle thematic coherence
discrimination, a gap that retrieval strength alone cannot explain.
Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{\bench{}}: 300 exhibitions, 23{,}658 objects across five open-access collections.
    \item \textbf{Three tasks}: \meip{} (10-way ranking, 1{,}409 queries), \tes{} (50-way, 283 queries),
          \ecd{} (pairwise coherence, 4 levels, 500 samples).
    \item \textbf{31 systems}: BM25/SBERT/RAG+KG retrieval baselines plus 21 frontier and 7 open-weight LLMs under unified zero-/few-shot protocol.
    \item \textbf{Four empirical findings}: heterogeneous few-shot gains on MEIP (up to $+4.1\%$, models diverge),

    \item \textbf{Two novel insights}: (i)~sample-size sensitivity---expanding MEIP from 500 to 1{,}409 queries

          overestimate model capability; (ii)~Chinese-developed LLMs (Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro) outperform
          all Western frontier APIs on MEIP, challenging assumptions about cultural knowledge distribution.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Exhibition Retrieval and Sequential Recommendation}

Dense passage retrieval \citep{karpukhin2020dpr} and MS-MARCO
\citep{nguyen2016msmarco} set standards for text ranking but lack
collection-level semantics.
Sequential coherence has been studied through playlist continuation
\citep{schedl2022music} and recipe completion \citep{bosselut2018simulating},
which share structural similarities with \meip{}.
\bench{} goes further: \ecd{} tests \emph{discriminative} coherence
judgment across four calibrated difficulty levels,
a formulation absent from all prior recommendation benchmarks.

\subsection{LLM Evaluation Benchmarks}

MMLU \citep{hendrycks2020mmlu} and BIG-Bench \citep{srivastava2022bigbench}
benchmark factual and reasoning capabilities across diverse domains,
but neither addresses curation, the multi-step assembly of culturally
coherent object sequences.
CulturalBench \citep{culturalbench2024} probes cultural knowledge via QA
but is single-task and single-source;
OK-VQA \citep{schwartz2022okvqa} targets visual question answering over
everyday objects with no exhibition-level semantics.
\citet{min2022rethinking} show that labels matter less than format in
few-shot settings; we extend this insight to curation and find that
frontier models exhibit heterogeneous integration regimes under
shuffled-label controls (H3, Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
Table~\ref{tab:related} summarises how \bench{} differs from the above
datasets across seven key axes.


\subsection{Cultural Bias in LLMs}

Prior work applies NER \citep{broscheit2010named}, deep learning for art analysis
\citep{cetinic2019deep}, and CLIP \citep{radford2021clip} to cultural
artefacts, but none define a curation task where exhibition-level coherence
is the evaluation criterion.
\citet{cao-etal-2023-assessing} and \citet{adilazuarda-etal-2024-towards}
audit LLM cultural knowledge; we operationalise bias \emph{quantitatively}
through per-group Hit@1 on MEIP, finding that the bias direction
depends on system type: retrieval baselines show Western preference,
while frontier LLMs show higher accuracy on non-Western objects,
suggesting that instruction tuning and cultural knowledge integration
shift---but do not eliminate---cultural performance disparities.

\section{Task Definitions}
\label{sec:tasks}

Let $\mathcal{O}$ be the set of all 23{,}658 objects, and let each
exhibition $e = (o_1, \ldots, o_m) \in \mathcal{O}^m$ be a sequence
of objects with associated theme $\tau_e$.

\subsection{Masked Exhibition Item Prediction (\meip{})}
\label{sec:meip}

\paragraph{Formulation.}
Given a context sequence $\mathbf{c} = (o_1,\ldots,o_{j-1},o_{j+1},\ldots,o_n)$
and a candidate set $\mathcal{C} = \{o^*\} \cup \mathcal{N}$ with
$|\mathcal{C}| = 10$, the model ranks $\mathcal{C}$ to minimise
$\pi(o^*)$.

\paragraph{Hard negatives.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}_e = \operatorname*{top}\text{-}9_{o \in \mathcal{O} \setminus e}
\; \mathrm{BM25}(\tau_e,\, o),
\label{eq:negatives}
\end{equation}
where BM25 scores concatenated \texttt{title} $\oplus$ \texttt{description}.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\begin{align}
\text{Acc@1}  &= \mathbf{1}[\pi(o^*) = 1], \label{eq:acc1}\\
\text{Hits@}k &= \mathbf{1}[\pi(o^*) \le k], \quad k \in \{3, 5\}, \label{eq:hitsk}\\
\text{MRR}    &= \tfrac{1}{\pi(o^*)}. \label{eq:mrr}
\end{align}
All metrics are macro-averaged over 1{,}409 queries.

\subsection{Theme-based Exhibition Selection (\tes{})}
\label{sec:tes}

\paragraph{Formulation.}
Given a thematic query $\tau$ and a candidate pool
$\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{G} \cup \mathcal{D}$ with $|\mathcal{P}| = 50$
(1 gold exhibition $+$ 49 distractors), the model ranks $\mathcal{P}$ to
retrieve $\mathcal{G}$.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\begin{align}
\text{NDCG@}k &= \frac{\mathrm{DCG@}k}{\mathrm{IDCG@}k}, \nonumber\
\mathrm{DCG@}k &= \sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mathrm{rel}_i}{\log_2(i+1)}.
    \label{eq:ndcg}
\end{align}
We also report $\text{P@}k = |R_k \cap \mathcal{G}|/k$ and $\text{Hits@1} = \mathbf{1}[o_1^* = \mathcal{G}]$.

\subsection{Exhibition Coherence Discrimination (\ecd{})}
\label{sec:ecd}

\paragraph{Formulation.}
Given two exhibition sequences $e^+$ (authentic) and $e^-$ (perturbed),
the model must identify $e^+$.
Formally, $f(e^+, e^-) \in \{0, 1\}$ where $1$ denotes correct
identification.

\paragraph{Perturbation levels (increasing difficulty).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt,leftmargin=1em]
  \item \textbf{L1 Temporal Anachronism}: one object replaced by an artefact dating $>$500 years from the exhibition mean.
  \item \textbf{L2 Cultural Drift}: replaced by an object from a different cultural group.
  \item \textbf{L3 Thematic Deviation}: replaced by a BM25 hard negative, topically related but not from the exhibition.
  \item \textbf{L4 Subtle Incoherence}: replaced by an SBERT top-20 neighbour with stylistic or provenance contradictions not visible from the title alone.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Per-level accuracy:
\begin{equation}
\text{PairAcc}_\ell = \frac{1}{|Q_\ell|} \sum_{q \in Q_\ell} \mathbf{1}[f_q = 1],
\quad \ell \in \{1,2,3,4\},
\label{eq:pairacc}
\end{equation}
and macro-average $\overline{\text{PairAcc}} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_\ell \text{PairAcc}_\ell$.
The benchmark constructs 200 samples per level (800 total); evaluations use a balanced 500-sample subset.

\section{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

We evaluate 24 models spanning retrieval baselines and proprietary frontier APIs
(Table~\ref{tab:models}).
All LLM baselines use identical zero-shot prompt templates;
few-shot variants are analysed in Section~\ref{sec:fewshot}.


\paragraph{Retrieval baselines.}
BM25 \citep{robertson1994okapi} ranks by term overlap;
SBERT ranks by cosine similarity to the mean context embedding;
RAG+KG appends CIDOC-CRM triples to the GPT-5.2 prompt.

\paragraph{Frontier LLMs.}
GPT-5/5.1/5.2, Claude Opus 4.5/4.6/Sonnet~4.5, Gemini~2.5--3.1, and
the DeepSeek/Doubao/Kimi/GLM/MiniMax families are evaluated under a
unified zero-shot prompt template.
Gemini~3.1~Pro achieves the best zero-shot MEIP (MRR~$0.685$) and ECD Macro ($0.876$);
Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro closely follows on MEIP (MRR~$0.642$).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
All LLMs are queried through a unified prompt template (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts})
with temperature $= 0$ and context-appropriate output length limits
(max\_tokens $= 512$ for ranking tasks; $= 4$ for ECD binary judgments).
Unless stated otherwise, all results use the zero-shot setting;
few-shot and chain-of-thought ablations follow in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}.

\paragraph{TES noleak protocol.}
To prevent candidate-label leakage in \tes{}, all 50 candidate exhibitions
are anonymised as \texttt{EX\_001}--\texttt{EX\_050}; their title, theme,
and description fields are masked, and only constituent object lists are
retained.
All reported TES results use this noleak variant;
the leaky baseline is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:leakage} for reference.

\paragraph{Retrieval baselines.}
BM25 (BM25Okapi), Sentence-BERT (all-mpnet-base-v2), and a
graph-augmented RAG+KG pipeline serve as non-LLM baselines on MEIP.
TES and ECD are not applicable to RAG+KG due to ranking and classification
format constraints.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
MEIP uses MRR and Hit@1 over 10-way candidate sets.
TES uses NDCG@10 and MRR over 50-way sets.
ECD reports per-level pairwise accuracy (PairAcc L1--L4) and their
unweighted macro average (Macro PairAcc).

The combined results table (Table~\ref{tab:main}) reveals several key findings.
On \textbf{MEIP}, Gemini~3.1~Pro achieves the best MRR ($0.685$) and Hit@1 ($0.589$),
followed by Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro ($0.642$ MRR) and Doubao-Seed-2.0-Lite ($0.626$ MRR).
All frontier LLMs substantially outperform BM25 ($0.556$ MRR) and SBERT ($0.647$ MRR),
confirming genuine curatorial reasoning beyond surface lexical matching.
DeepSeek-R1 ($0.529$ MRR) underperforms despite chain-of-thought,
suggesting extended reasoning hurts rapid object-selection tasks.
Gemini~3~Pro achieves MRR $0.820$ but on a 500-query subset only,
making direct comparison with the full 1{,}409-query evaluation infeasible;
its ranking should be interpreted with caution.
A striking finding is that the Doubao-Seed family---particularly
Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro---outperforms all GPT and Claude variants on MEIP,
suggesting that Chinese-developed LLMs may possess competitive or superior
cultural object retrieval capabilities for museum contexts.

\subsection{TES Results}

On \textbf{TES}, Claude Opus 4.6 achieves the highest NDCG@10 $= 0.437$ under the
anonymised, leak-free protocol (Table~\ref{tab:main}), closely followed by
Claude Sonnet 4.5 ($0.418$), Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro ($0.410$),
Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini 2.5 Pro (both $\approx 0.408$), and GPT-5.1 ($0.406$).
Notably, SBERT achieves NDCG@10 $= 0.441$, above most frontier LLMs, suggesting
dense semantic retrieval is competitive when candidate label leakage is eliminated.
Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro, previously anomalously high at $0.703$ under the leaky protocol,
now scores $0.410$, confirming that the original high score was a leakage artefact
(Appendix~\ref{app:leakage}).
DeepSeek-R1 ($0.343$) performs substantially below instruction-tuned models.

\paragraph{TES few-shot results.}
Few-shot demonstrations yield only modest gains on TES (+0--2 NDCG@10 pp),
in sharp contrast to large MEIP improvements.
Frontier models show marginal gains (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro: 0.407→0.417 at 1-shot)
or slight regression (Claude Sonnet 4.5: 0.418→0.408 at 3-shot), suggesting the
50-way ranking format is harder to calibrate via demonstrations.
Kimi-K2.5 (0.109/0.112 shot1/3), GLM-5 (0.148/0.161), and MiniMax-M2.5 (0.197/0.181)
score far below the frontier cluster at all shot counts,
indicating persistent difficulty with the anonymised 50-way format
(full results in Appendix~\ref{app:fewshot_full}).

\subsection{ECD Results}

On \textbf{ECD}, frontier models excel on coarse perturbations (L1/L2:
$0.49$--$0.98$) but struggle near-chance at L3 Thematic Deviation
($0.45$--$0.63$), before partially recovering at L4 ($0.50$--$0.98$).
This U-shaped profile confirms that \emph{distinguishing topically similar
but curatorially distinct objects} is the hardest capability for current LLMs.
Gemini~3.1~Pro leads overall (Macro $0.876$); the Doubao-Seed family
follows ($0.852$--$0.856$).
DeepSeek-R1 and Kimi-K2.5 score near-random across all levels ($0.486$/$0.498$),
as chain-of-thought provides no benefit for binary discrimination.
DeepSeek-V3 achieves the highest L3 score ($0.632$),
suggesting dense instruction-tuned architectures have marginal advantages
for thematic coherence detection.

\paragraph{Open-weight models.}
Among open-weight models, Qwen2.5-72B leads on MEIP (MRR $0.733$ on 500-query subset),
ECD Macro ($0.674$), and overall, demonstrating that large instruction-tuned models can
be competitive on object-level curation tasks.
However, its MEIP score should be compared cautiously with frontier models evaluated
on the full 1{,}409 queries, as the 500-query subset overestimates performance.
Llama-3.1-70B achieves the highest TES NDCG@10 among open-weight models ($0.437$),
matching Claude Opus 4.6, a striking result given the 10$\times$ parameter gap to frontier APIs.
Smaller models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B) fall substantially below the 70B tier across all tasks,
confirming that cultural curatorial reasoning benefits from scale.
Qwen3-8B achieves surprisingly high MEIP (MRR $0.595$, Hit@1 $0.498$) and TES ($0.382$)
for an 8B model, outperforming some frontier models on a per-parameter basis,
suggesting that recent architectural improvements (e.g., thinking-mode compatibility)
benefit smaller models disproportionately.

\subsection{Few-shot Behaviour: Three Mechanistic Hypotheses}
\label{sec:fewshot}

\paragraph{H1: Cultural Anchoring.}
\label{h1}
\emph{Hypothesis}: Demonstrations introduce cultural bias,
depressing performance on queries whose gold objects differ culturally
from the demonstration examples.
We select Western-themed (Met/AIC) and Eastern-themed (V\&A East Asia)
demonstration pairs on a 182-sample subset; results are in Appendix~\ref{app:h1}.
\emph{Finding}: The Eastern advantage at zero-shot is a dataset property
(Eastern objects come from curated V\&A collections with richer thematic
metadata), not an artefact of demonstration content.
Western demonstrations do not systematically widen the West--East gap;
per-demo-type MRR variation stays within $\pm$0.03 for all four models,
ruling out cultural anchoring as the primary driver of few-shot gains.

\paragraph{H2: Few-Shot Benefit and Capability Tier.}
\label{h2}
\emph{Hypothesis}: In-context demonstrations provide genuine curatorial signal;
stronger models extract it more effectively.
Table~\ref{tab:shotablation} reveals a nuanced picture:
\textbf{Gemini-2.5-Pro} shows the clearest few-shot gain
($+0.025$ MRR at 3-shot, $+4.1\%$ relative),
while \textbf{GPT-5.2} is essentially flat ($0.619{\to}0.624$, $+0.8\%$),
suggesting it already nears its ceiling on the full query set.
\textbf{DeepSeek-V3.2} peaks modestly at 1-shot ($+0.014$) then regresses,
consistent with limited few-shot capacity.
\textbf{Claude-Opus-4.6} improves steadily ($0.621{\to}0.631$, $+1.6\%$),
reflecting a high but improvable zero-shot baseline.
Gemini~3~Pro shows strong and consistent performance across shot counts
(MRR $0.820$--$0.827$ on 500 queries), confirming that the Gemini~3 series has
robust zero-shot curatorial reasoning.
Notably, all few-shot gains on the full 1{,}409-query evaluation are substantially
smaller than those observed on the 500-query subset, highlighting the
importance of full-scale evaluation for reliable few-shot analysis.

\subsection{Cultural Bias Audit}
\label{sec:cultural-bias}

\paragraph{Methodology.}
We evaluate 7 frontier LLMs and SBERT on 200 \meip{} samples with known cultural provenance.
Objects are assigned to \textsc{Western} ($n{=}105$), \textsc{Asian} ($n{=}10$),
and \textsc{Islamic} ($n{=}5$) groups via keyword matching on \texttt{culture} metadata;
the remaining 80 objects are labelled \textsc{Other/Ancient}.
We define a cultural-bias statistic:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{W{-}NW}} =
  \text{Hit@1}_{\textsc{Western}} -
  \tfrac{\text{Hit@1}_{\textsc{Asian}}
        + \text{Hit@1}_{\textsc{Islamic}}}{2}.
\label{eq:delta-wnw}
\end{equation}
Positive $\Delta$ indicates Western preference; negative $\Delta$ indicates higher accuracy on non-Western objects.

\paragraph{Findings.}
Table~\ref{tab:cultural-bias} reveals a pattern that \emph{reverses the expected direction}:
\emph{all seven frontier LLMs exhibit negative $\Delta_{\mathrm{W{-}NW}}$}
(range: $-0.045$ to $-0.240$), meaning they score \emph{higher} on Asian and Islamic
objects than on Western ones.
Only SBERT shows a positive $\Delta$ ($+0.068$), consistent with
Western-art-terminology dominance in English training corpora.
We attribute the LLM reversal to two factors.
First, the non-Western groups have very small sample sizes
(Asian $n{=}10$, Islamic $n{=}5$), making per-group Hit@1 estimates
unreliable (confidence intervals overlap substantially); the negative
$\Delta$ should be interpreted cautiously.
Second, Asian and Islamic objects in our corpus tend to come from
well-curated V\&A and Cleveland Museum collections with richer thematic
metadata, making them easier to identify regardless of cultural background.
Despite the reversal, \textbf{Islamic objects score lowest for most models}
(Hit@1 $0.40$--$0.80$, but with $n{=}5$),
while Asian objects perform moderately to well ($0.30$--$0.70$).
The key takeaway is that \emph{cultural bias in curation is more nuanced
than a simple Western-preference narrative}: corpus curation quality,
metadata richness, and sample size all confound the measurement,
and benchmark designers should report group sizes and confidence intervals
alongside bias statistics.

\subsection{Error Pattern Analysis}
\label{sec:errors}

Manual inspection of 200 MEIP error cases (Appendix~\ref{app:errors}) reveals five failure modes.
Two are novel: \textbf{Semantic Overlap without Curation Logic}
(SBERT similarity $>0.7$ but wrong rank, 32--47\% of errors)
and \textbf{Popularity Bias} (predicted objects appear $\ge 3\times$ more in training data, 20--27\%).
Together they account for the majority of errors previously labelled ``Other'' in three-category schemes.
Semantic Overlap dominates for LLMs, confirming that models recognise topical relevance
but miss higher-order curatorial selection logic.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Sample-Size Sensitivity and Benchmark Reliability}
\label{sec:samplesize}

A striking observation from our full-scale evaluation is the substantial
gap between the 500-query subset and the complete 1{,}409-query MEIP evaluation.
When expanding from 500 to 1{,}409 queries, frontier MRR drops by
12--21\% (e.g., GPT-5.2: $0.746 \to 0.619$, $-17\%$; Claude Opus 4.6:
$0.781 \to 0.621$, $-20\%$; Gemini 3.1 Pro: $0.827 \to 0.685$, $-17\%$),
while the relative model ranking also shifts.
This has two implications for benchmark design:
(i)~\emph{small-benchmark evaluations systematically overestimate model
capability}, as the easier queries tend to be completed first;
(ii)~\emph{model rankings are not stable across sample sizes},
meaning conclusions drawn from partial evaluations may not hold at scale.
We therefore recommend that future LLM benchmarks report results at
multiple sample sizes and flag when rankings change, a practice
rarely adopted in current benchmark literature.

\subsection{Chinese-Developed LLMs Excel at Cultural Object Retrieval}
\label{sec:chinese-llm}

An unexpected finding from the full evaluation is that the
Doubao-Seed family---particularly Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro
(MRR $0.642$, Hit@1 $0.539$)---outperforms all GPT variants
(GPT-5.2 MRR $0.619$) and Claude models
(Claude Opus 4.6 MRR $0.621$) on MEIP, despite being primarily
trained on Chinese-language data.
This advantage does not extend to TES (Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro NDCG@10 $0.410$
vs.\ Claude Opus 4.6 $0.437$) or ECD (Macro $0.852$ vs.\ $0.836$),
suggesting that Chinese LLMs' strength lies in object-level
retrieval rather than exhibition-level coherence reasoning.
We hypothesise that the rich cultural artefact metadata in Chinese
training corpora (museum digitalisation initiatives, cultural heritage
databases) may provide denser object-level knowledge, even for Western art,
while exhibition-level narrative reasoning remains a general challenge.
This finding challenges the assumption that Western-developed LLMs
inherently dominate English-language cultural tasks.

\subsection{The Ranking--Discrimination Disconnect}
\label{sec:rank-disc}

Comparing MEIP and ECD performance reveals a \emph{ranking--discrimination
disconnect}: models that rank objects well do not necessarily judge
coherence well.
The Spearman correlation between MEIP MRR and ECD Macro across all
24 evaluated systems is $\rho = 0.71$, but this is driven largely by
the very weak models (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-K2.5) that fail on both tasks.
Among the top-performing cluster, the relationship reverses:
Gemini 3.1 Pro leads on both tasks, but Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro
outperforms GPT-5.2 on MEIP while matching it on ECD,
and Gemini 3 Pro achieves the highest MEIP (on 500 queries)
but only average ECD (Macro $0.644$).
This disconnect suggests that \emph{object-level retrieval and
coherence discrimination are partially orthogonal capabilities},
and that benchmarks evaluating only one dimension risk providing
an incomplete picture of curatorial AI competence.

\subsection{Demo Interface}
\label{sec:demo}

To facilitate qualitative inspection of model behaviour,
we built an interactive demonstration system using NiceGUI
that allows curators and researchers to explore \bench{} queries
in real time.
The interface is organised into three task tabs mirroring the benchmark:
\meip{} (masked item completion), \ecd{} (coherence detection),
and \tes{} (theme-based exhibition retrieval).
Users select a benchmark sample from the dataset, choose a model from
the 21 evaluated LLMs, and receive the model's ranked candidates or
binary judgment within seconds via the same LiteLLM gateway used in
our evaluation.
Candidate objects are displayed side-by-side with their museum metadata,
enabling curators to compare model selections against ground truth.
User interactions and free-text comments are logged to
\texttt{logs/feedback\_nicegui.jsonl}, providing a lightweight
annotation stream for future human evaluation collection.
The system is released alongside the benchmark codebase and runs
at \texttt{localhost:7861}.

\subsection{Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human-eval}

a top-ranked object can be domain-consistent yet narratively inappropriate.
To measure this gap, three domain-expert museum curators independently
evaluated outputs from the top-three LLMs (GPT-5.2, Claude~Opus~4.6,

\subsection{Effect of Domain-Specific Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:finetune}

To investigate whether domain-specific supervision can improve small open-weight models on \bench{}, we fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B on MEIP training data using LoRA (rank 64, $\alpha{=}128$, learning rate $2{\times}10^{-5}$, 3 epochs).
Training samples are constructed from the 200 held-out exhibitions not overlapping with the test split: for each query, the gold item is treated as the preferred response and a randomly sampled hard negative as the rejected response, yielding 2{,}400 pairwise preference pairs.
We apply DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) to encourage the model to rank gold items higher.

These results confirm that \bench{} is \emph{actionable}: even lightweight fine-tuning on benchmark-derived data yields meaningful performance gains, motivating future work on culturally balanced curation-specific training.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \bench{}, a multi-source, triple-task benchmark for
evaluating LLMs as museum curation assistants.
Across 300 exhibitions, 23{,}658 objects, three tasks,
and 28 evaluated systems (21 frontier + 7 open-weight),
our evaluation reveals four systematic findings:
(1)~few-shot demonstrations yield heterogeneous MEIP gains
(Gemini-2.5-Pro $+4.1\%$; GPT-5.2 saturated at $+0.8\%$;
H3 shuffled-label experiments reveal four distinct regimes, from
pre-trained-prior reliance in GPT-5.2 to deep content integration in Gemini-2.5-Pro);
(2)~chain-of-thought hurts ECD universally ($-14$ to $-34$ pp) and collapses
Claude-Opus-4.6 and DeepSeek-V3.2 MEIP due to format non-compliance,
confirming CoT benefits are task- and model-specific on \bench{};
(3)~cultural bias is more nuanced than previously assumed: LLMs show negative
$\Delta_{\mathrm{W{-}NW}}$ (higher accuracy on non-Western objects) while
SBERT shows positive $\Delta$ ($+0.068$), suggesting corpus quality and
sample size confound bias measurement;
(4)~a novel thematic coherence gap (\ecd{} L3 near-chance across all models, range $0.45$--$0.63$,
revealing that frontier LLMs handle coarse mismatches but fail on topic-relevant
intruder detection).
Additionally, expanding from 500 to 1{,}409 MEIP queries reduced frontier MRR by
12--21\%, demonstrating that small-benchmark evaluations substantially
overestimate curatorial capability and underscoring the necessity of
full-scale evaluation for reliable benchmarking.
We release all data, code, and baseline implementations to support
future work on culturally aware AI systems for heritage institutions.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{ECD L4 validity.}
L4 uses SBERT top-20 neighbours as intruders, partially confounding SBERT coherence scores;
future work should use human-validated examples.

\paragraph{Cultural label quality.}
Keyword matching assigns ``Other'' to 55.6\% of objects due to sparser metadata in AIC and Europeana records, inflating the ``Unknown'' group in bias analyses.

\paragraph{Multimodal signals.}
\bench{} is text-only; visual attributes (iconography, colour, texture)
central to real-world curation remain outside the current formulation.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

All data are CC0 or Public Domain; no personally identifiable information is included.
Cultural group labels are approximate keyword-derived proxies
and should not replace expert classification.
The cultural performance disparities (Section~\ref{sec:cultural-bias}) represent real risks
of representational harm if deployed without human oversight, regardless of the bias direction.

\section{Data Collection Algorithm}
\label{app:algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\small
\caption{ExhibitionBench data collection and filtering}
\label{alg:collect}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Raw exhibition records $\mathcal{E}$, object metadata APIs
\Ensure Aligned benchmark dataset $\mathcal{D}$
\State $\mathcal{D} \gets \emptyset$
\ForAll{source $s \in \{$Met, Europeana, AIC, CMA, V\&A$\}$}
    \ForAll{exhibition $e \in \mathcal{E}_s$}
        \State Fetch object metadata for all items in $e$
        \If{$|e| < 5$} \State \textbf{continue} \EndIf
        \ForAll{object $o \in e$}
            \If{$o$ lacks \texttt{title}} \State Remove $o$ from $e$ \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \If{$|e| \ge 5$} \State Add $e$ to $\mathcal{D}$ \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Deduplicate objects by (source, id) across exhibitions
\State \Return $\mathcal{D}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\texttt{You are a museum curator. Given the following exhibition theme

Theme: \{theme\}\\
Context objects: \{context\_objects\}\\
Candidates (10 items): \{candidate\_list\}\\

\begin{quote}
\texttt{You are a museum curator selecting exhibitions by theme.\\
Query theme: \{theme\}\\
Query description: \{description\}\\
Candidates (exhibitions described by their artworks):\\
\{candidate\_list\}\\
Each candidate is identified by an anonymous ID (EX\_001, EX\_002, \ldots).\\
Judge solely by the artworks inside each exhibition.\\
Rank the top 10 exhibitions most suitable for this theme.
Output only the ranked list of anonymous IDs, one per line.}
\end{quote}

Two exhibition sequences are shown below.

Sequence A: \{sequence\_a\}\\
Sequence B: \{sequence\_b\}\\
Which sequence is more coherent? Reply with only ``A'' or ``B''.}

\paragraph{Image input ablation (\meip{}).}
We investigate whether providing artwork images alongside metadata
benefits model performance on the MEIP task.
For six frontier models we run two conditions:
(i)~\textbf{text-only} (the default protocol), and
(ii)~\textbf{text+image}, where each context and candidate object is
accompanied by its collection image whenever available from a
CDN-accessible URL (Met~Museum, Europeana, Cleveland~Art, V\&A,
Wikimedia Commons; coverage $\approx$60--80\% of MEIP objects).

Table~\ref{tab:vision-ablation} shows the results.
Across all six models the average MRR change is only $+0.003$,
with three models improving and three degrading.
Only Gemini~2.5~Flash shows a noteworthy gain ($+0.039$ MRR,
$+0.050$ H@1); all other models change by less than 2~pp.
We attribute the general null or negative effect to two factors:
(a)~artwork images are largely uninformative for the
\emph{contextual-fit} reasoning required by MEIP: a Tang-dynasty
jar looks like a jar regardless of whether its image is shown;
(b)~embedding $\sim$8--15 low-resolution thumbnails per prompt
inflates the effective token count and may distract attention from
the textual metadata that carries the curatorial signal.
These results reinforce the benchmark's core premise:
the bottleneck is \emph{cultural anchoring knowledge},
not perceptual recognition.

\section{TES Design: Preventing Candidate Label Leakage}
\label{app:leakage}

\paragraph{Problem.}
An early version of the TES task exposed four redundant leakage signals
in each candidate exhibition record:
(i)~the \texttt{theme} field was an exact string match to \texttt{query\_theme};
(ii)~the \texttt{title} field contained the theme keyword verbatim;
(iii)~the \texttt{id} field encoded the theme (e.g., \texttt{met\_impressionism});
(iv)~the \texttt{description} field paraphrased the theme.
A lexical BM25 baseline exploiting these fields achieved NDCG@10\,$=0.979$ on
the full 283-query test set (trivially near-perfect), confirming the leakage.
Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro and similar instruction-tuned models that apply aggressive
keyword matching also achieve anomalously high TES scores under this setup.

\paragraph{Fix.}
We redesigned the TES candidate representation to expose \emph{only}
artwork-level content.
Specifically, the anonymised prompt:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Replaces each real candidate ID (\texttt{met\_impressionism}) with an
        anonymous token (\texttt{EX\_001}, \texttt{EX\_002}, \dots);
  \item Strips \texttt{theme}, \texttt{title}, and \texttt{description} entirely;
  \item Retains up to five \texttt{sample\_objects} per candidate exhibition,
        each described as \texttt{<artwork title> (<culture>, <date>)}.
\end{itemize}
Models must now identify the best-matching exhibitions solely by recognising
artwork-level cultural and stylistic patterns, the intended curatorial
reasoning task.
Under this leak-free protocol all evaluated models drop substantially,
with the best LLM (Claude Opus~4.6) reaching NDCG@10\,$=0.437$, well below the
original BM25 near-ceiling.
All TES results reported in the main tables use the anonymised protocol.
